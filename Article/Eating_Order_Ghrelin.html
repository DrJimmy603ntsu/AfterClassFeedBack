Effect of Food Order on Ghrelin Suppression

Alpana P. Shukla, Elizabeth Mauer, Leon I. Igel, Wanda Truong, Anthony Casper, Rekha B. Kumar, Katherine H. Saunders, and Louis J. Aronne

Comprehensive Weight Control Center, Division of Endocrinology, Diabetes and Metabolism, Weill Cornell Medicine, New York, NY  |  Department of Healthcare Policy and Research, Weill Cornell Medicine, New York, NY

Received 25 October 2017 · Accepted 6 February 2018 · © 2018 American Diabetes Association

Key Finding This is the first study to demonstrate that manipulation of macronutrient order during a meal can impact gut hormone excursions. Eating carbohydrates last maintained ghrelin suppression at 180 min, whereas eating carbohydrates first allowed ghrelin to rebound to pre-meal levels — a difference that may have implications for satiety and weight regulation.

Background

The temporal sequence of carbohydrate ingestion during a meal has a significant impact on postprandial glucose, insulin, and GLP-1 excursions in type 2 diabetes. However, the effects of food order on ghrelin suppression and satiety had not previously been reported.

Ghrelin is the only known orexigenic (hunger-stimulating) gut peptide, while GLP-1 is one of several anorexigenic (satiety-inducing) signaling hormones. Understanding how meal composition and order affect these hormones may offer practical dietary strategies for appetite and weight control.

Study Design

Using a crossover design, 16 subjects with overweight/obesity and metformin-treated type 2 diabetes consumed the same meal on 3 separate days in random order. All three meals contained identical foods and calories — only the eating sequence differed.

No significant effect of food order was observed on subjective VAS appetite measures (hunger or fullness ratings) across the three meal conditions.

Discussion

Prior work by this group established that the carbohydrate-last meal pattern reduces postprandial glucose excursions. Controlling for carbohydrate amount, this meal pattern also stimulates lower insulin and higher GLP-1 response compared with the carbohydrate-first pattern.

Taken together with the new findings on ghrelin suppression, macronutrient order during a meal modifies the pattern of postprandial insulin and gut hormone secretion, which could potentially impact satiety and weight regulation. However, consistent with earlier studies investigating isocaloric meal interventions, these disparate hormonal responses did not translate into significant differences in subjective hunger/satiety scores.

The authors note two likely explanations for this dissociation: (1) the inherent subjectivity and limitations of the VAS tool for capturing appetite, and (2) the complexity of appetite regulation — ghrelin is the only known hunger-promoting peptide, whereas GLP-1 is just one of several satiety-signaling gut hormones.

Further study with extended observation periods, assessment of additional gut hormones, meals with varied macronutrient composition, and more objective satiety measures is needed before firm clinical recommendations can be made.

Conclusion

This is the first study to demonstrate that manipulation of macronutrient order can impact gut hormone excursions — specifically ghrelin suppression. Eating protein and vegetables before carbohydrates maintains ghrelin suppression for at least 3 hours post-meal, whereas eating carbohydrates first allows ghrelin to rebound to pre-meal levels by 180 minutes. The clinical implications for satiety and weight management require further study.
