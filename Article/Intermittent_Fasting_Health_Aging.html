Effects of Intermittent Fasting on Health, Aging, and Disease

Rafael de Cabo, Ph.D., and Mark P. Mattson, Ph.D.

Translational Gerontology Branch, Intramural Research Program, National Institute on Aging, NIH, Baltimore  |  Laboratory of Neurosciences, NIA, NIH, Baltimore  |  Department of Neuroscience, Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore

© 2019 Massachusetts Medical Society  |  Last updated February 12, 2020, at NEJM.org

Article Overview This review synthesizes preclinical and clinical evidence on how intermittent fasting (IF) affects metabolism, cellular stress resistance, aging, and chronic disease. The authors describe the three main IF regimens, the biology of metabolic switching, and clinical outcomes across obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and neurological disorders. Practical prescribing guidance is provided.

Background

Caloric restriction has long been known to extend life span in animals. What was less recognized historically is that rodents on caloric restriction typically consume their entire daily food allotment within a few hours, creating a daily fasting window of up to 20 hours during which ketogenesis occurs. Since the late 1990s, hundreds of animal studies and scores of clinical trials have examined controlled intermittent fasting regimens, in which metabolic switching — from liver-derived glucose to adipose cell–derived ketones — occurs daily or several days per week.

Many health benefits of intermittent fasting are not simply the result of reduced free-radical production or weight loss. Instead, intermittent fasting elicits evolutionarily conserved, adaptive cellular responses that improve glucose regulation, increase stress resistance, and suppress inflammation. Most people today consume three meals a day plus snacks; as a result, intermittent fasting does not naturally occur.

The Three Main Intermittent-Fasting Regimens

Alternate-Day Fasting

Alternating 24-hour periods of eating and fasting (or severe restriction to 500–700 kcal). Studied extensively for weight loss, cardiometabolic outcomes, and longevity in animals and humans.

5:2 Intermittent Fasting

Eat normally 5 days per week; restrict to 500–700 kcal on 2 non-consecutive days. Produces similar weight loss to daily caloric restriction but with greater gains in insulin sensitivity.

Time-Restricted Feeding

Limit all food intake to a 6–8 hour window daily, creating a 16–18 hour fasting period. A practical entry point for patients; can be adopted gradually over several months.

Intermittent Fasting and Metabolic Switching

After meals, glucose is used for energy and fat is stored as triglycerides. During fasting, triglycerides are broken down to fatty acids and glycerol. The liver converts fatty acids to ketone bodies (acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate, β-HB), which provide a major energy source for many tissues — especially the brain.

In rodents, ketone elevation begins within 4–8 h and reaches millimolar levels within 24 h.

Ketone bodies are not merely fuel — they are potent signaling molecules that regulate PGC-1α, FGF21, NAD⁺, sirtuins, PARP1, and CD38, profoundly affecting systemic metabolism. β-HB also stimulates expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), with implications for brain health and neurodegenerative disorders.

Several benefits of intermittent fasting are dissociated from weight loss itself, including improvements in glucose regulation, blood pressure, heart rate, endurance training efficacy, and abdominal fat loss.

Cellular and Molecular Mechanisms

Intermittent fasting triggers a coordinated three-phase biological response. During fasting, cells enter a stress-resistance and repair mode. During recovery (eating and sleeping), cells shift to growth and plasticity. Over the long term, these alternating cycles produce durable adaptations that improve health and disease resistance.

Intermittent fasting stimulates autophagy and mitophagy while inhibiting the mTOR protein-synthesis pathway — enabling cells to remove oxidatively damaged proteins and mitochondria and recycle molecular constituents. These pathways are suppressed in persons who overeat and are sedentary.

Intermittent Fasting and Stress Resistance

Unlike modern humans, our ancestors did not consume three regularly spaced large meals plus snacks every day, nor did they live sedentary lives. Homo sapiens evolved in ecologic niches where food sources were sparsely distributed, which drove adaptations including creativity, language, and physical endurance.

Most if not all organ systems respond to intermittent fasting in ways that enable the organism to tolerate challenges and restore homeostasis. Repeated fasting results in lasting adaptive responses — increased expression of antioxidant defenses, DNA repair enzymes, protein quality-control machinery, mitochondrial biogenesis, autophagy, and down-regulation of inflammation. These adaptive responses to fasting and feeding are conserved across species.

Effects on Health and Aging

A meta-analysis of data from 1934–2012 showed that caloric restriction increases median life span by 14–45% in rats, but only 4–27% in mice, with wide variation by strain, sex, age, and diet. In a landmark study, alternate-day feeding increased the average life span of rats by up to 80% when started in young adulthood.

Two major monkey studies produced discrepant results: the University of Wisconsin study showed positive effects of caloric restriction on both health and survival, while the NIA study showed no significant reduction in mortality despite clear health improvements. Differences in diet composition, onset, caloric intake, sex, and genetic background likely explain the discrepancy.

In humans, intermittent-fasting interventions ameliorate obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and inflammation — to a greater extent than attributable to caloric reduction alone.

Practical Prescriptions for Clinicians

Despite strong evidence, three main barriers limit adoption: (1) culturally ingrained 3-meal-plus-snacks eating patterns; (2) initial side effects (hunger, irritability, reduced concentration) that typically resolve within 1 month; and (3) most physicians are not trained to prescribe IF regimens.

Monitoring should include regular tracking of body weight, fasting glucose, and ketone levels. A dietitian or nutritionist should be consulted to ensure nutritional needs are met. Physicians should provide ongoing communication, support, and positive reinforcement throughout the transition period.

Key clinical indications for IF prescription: obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, cancers, and conditions associated with overeating and a sedentary lifestyle.

Conclusions and Future Directions

Preclinical studies and clinical trials show that intermittent fasting has broad-spectrum benefits for obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancers, and neurological disorders. Animal models show that IF improves health throughout the life span; clinical studies have mainly involved shorter-term interventions in overweight young and middle-aged adults.

Key unanswered questions: Can people maintain IF for years and accrue the benefits seen in animals? Do these findings generalize to older adults, children, and lean individuals?

The beneficial effects of intermittent fasting involve metabolic switching and cellular stress resistance. For people who cannot adhere to IF, further understanding of these pathways may enable development of targeted pharmacologic therapies that mimic IF's effects. Candidate agents include metabolic challengers (metformin, 2-deoxyglucose), mitochondrial bioenergetic boosters (ketone esters, nicotinamide riboside), and mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus) — though available animal data suggest pharmacologic approaches are likely inferior to intermittent fasting itself.
